Operon v0.21: Adaptive Foundations

Pattern Repository, Watcher Component, Adaptive Assembly, and an Intervention Experience Pool

Bogdan Banu · March 2026 · github.com/coredipper/operon

Release: v0.21.0 – v0.21.1
Abstract

After adding temporal epistemics in v0.19 and wiring them into the runtime in v0.20, v0.21 gives Operon the ability to learn from past runs and intervene when execution degrades. The design draws on Dupoux, LeCun, and Malik’s “Why AI Systems Don’t Learn” (arXiv:2603.15381), which proposes a three-system cognitive architecture (System A for observation, System B for action, and System M for meta-control) bootstrapped through an evolutionary-developmental loop. This release maps those pieces onto Operon: PatternLibrary serves as evolutionary memory, WatcherComponent implements System M, and run-loop interventions form the meta-action surface. Together they provide the scaffolding for Phase 4’s dynamic assembly (completed in v0.21.1), where Operon starts choosing its own structure.

1. What Was Missing

After v0.20, Operon could run explicit multi-stage workflows, attach telemetry, and maintain auditable bi-temporal state. It still could not remember which workflow shapes succeeded on which kinds of tasks, or detect and recover from runtime degradation without manual oversight.

In biological terms, the organism had structure and memory, but neither an immune response nor evolutionary selection. A cell that cannot spot stagnation or retain lessons from past failures breaks down quickly under production conditions.

2. Why AI Systems Don’t Learn

The theoretical basis for v0.21 is a March 2026 paper by Emmanuel Dupoux (EHESS / Meta FAIR), Yann LeCun (NYU / Meta FAIR), and Jitendra Malik (UC Berkeley / Meta FAIR): “Why AI Systems Don’t Learn and What to Do About It: Lessons on Autonomous Learning from Cognitive Science.” They argue that hyperscaling text-based LLMs runs into structural limits that additional data and compute alone will not resolve. Deployed AI systems are static: learning stops the moment training ends, and every subsequent adaptation depends on human engineers.

Three Roadblocks

The paper identifies three structural obstacles to autonomous learning in current AI systems:

  1. Conceptual fragmentation. Observation-based learning (self-supervised learning on text, images, and video) and action-based learning (reinforcement learning through trial and error) live in separate pipelines, training recipes, and research communities. Biological organisms integrate both from birth.
  2. Externalization of learning. Human experts handle the entire MLOps loop (data sourcing, curation, loss design, training orchestration, benchmarking, and monitoring). Once deployed, the model behaves as a fixed function, whereas biological organisms regulate their own learning continuously without outside supervision.
  3. No scalable construction method. Bootstrapping a multi-component learning architecture runs into a circular dependency: the meta-controller needs experience generated by the learners, while the learners need the meta-controller to guide their updates. Current AI practice lacks a standard way to break this cycle.

System A: Learning from Observation

System A covers passive statistical learning from sensory streams. In cognitive science, this is the infant picking up visual and auditory regularities (discriminating faces, locating phonetic boundaries, and building intuitive physics) without explicit instruction. In AI, the closest analogue is self-supervised learning (SSL), where models trained on large corpora of text, images, or video predict masked inputs, reconstruct corrupted signals, or align augmented views.

System A scales well with data and learns abstract, hierarchical representations that transfer across downstream tasks. Its two main limits are that it cannot curate its own training signal (it consumes whatever data humans supply) and that it struggles to separate correlation from causation: noticing that umbrellas appear during rain does not establish which causes which.

Operon Mapping: System A → Fast Nucleus

In Operon, System A maps to fast_nucleus stages (mode="fixed"): cheap pattern-matching steps such as routing, classification, and extraction. They act as the organism’s perceptual layer, processing input quickly without extended deliberation or goal pursuit.

System B: Learning from Action

System B is active, goal-directed learning through interaction with an environment, like a toddler learning to walk by trying, falling, and adjusting motor commands in response to feedback. Its nearest counterpart in AI is reinforcement learning (RL), where agents learn policies by maximizing reward through trial and error.

Because System B is grounded in interaction, it can find solutions that passive observation misses. The trade-off is high sample complexity, difficulty scaling to large action spaces, and reliance on well-specified reward functions, which are often the hardest part of a problem to formulate.

Operon Mapping: System B → Deep Nucleus

In Operon, System B maps to deep_nucleus stages (mode="fuzzy") that handle planning, synthesis, and evaluation. This is the organism’s executive layer: slower and more expensive, reserved for weighing trade-offs and pursuing open-ended goals.

How A and B Interact

Neither system is sufficient on its own; each supplies what the other lacks:

  • A helps B by supplying compressed representations (keeping the RL agent off raw pixels), predictive world models for planning, and intrinsic reward signals such as prediction error, novelty, and uncertainty to steer exploration.
  • B helps A by selecting informative inputs for self-supervised learning (directing attention toward complex or uncertain stimuli) and generating grounded, task-relevant experience through goal-directed behavior.

In Operon, the SkillOrganism run loop provides this coupling. Fast stages produce routing signals, classifications, and summaries for deep stages, and deep stages produce outputs that shape subsequent fast stages, combining cheap perception and expensive deliberation in a single workflow.

System M: The Meta-Controller

The paper’s central proposal is System M, an autonomous meta-controller (comparable to executive control in the prefrontal cortex or the control plane in software-defined networking) that orchestrates learning itself. It tracks low-dimensional “meta-states” and issues “meta-actions” to route data between System A and System B.

The meta-states fall into three categories:

CategoryExamplesBiological Analogue
Epistemic Prediction error, uncertainty, learning gain, surprise Orienting reflex, curiosity, “aha” moments
Species-specific Direct gaze, looming stimuli, threat signatures Innate fear responses, social attention biases
Somatic Energy levels, pain, fatigue, homeostatic deviations Metabolic regulation, fight-or-flight, sleep pressure

From these signals, System M chooses meta-actions: connecting or disconnecting learning modules, switching between operating modes (learning, inference, optimization), supplying internal rewards or training targets, and enabling or suppressing data streams. The paper hypothesizes that System M’s core routing policy is largely hardwired by evolution rather than learned from scratch during an individual agent’s lifetime.

Operon Mapping: System M → WatcherComponent

WatcherComponent is Operon’s concrete instantiation of System M. It monitors EpiplexityMonitor (epistemic), ATP_Store (somatic), and ImmuneSystem (species-specific), then issues meta-actions: RETRY, ESCALATE, or HALT. Its base policy is explicitly configured, mirroring a hardwired System M; the Phase 4 experience pool (covered in §9) adds learned refinement on top of that fixed policy.

The Evo-Devo Framework

To bootstrap the A-B-M architecture and break the circular dependency between System M and Systems A and B, the paper separates evolution and development across two timescales, formalized as bilevel optimization:

  • Inner loop (development): During a single lifetime, the agent’s architecture (A, B, M) is initialized from meta-parameters φ. Systems A and B update through interaction with the environment under a fixed System M.
  • Outer loop (evolution): Across many lifetimes, meta-parameters φ are optimized against a fitness function evaluated over full life cycles in simulated environments, acting as selection on the genome.

The genome φ specifies initial weights for A and B, the routing policy of M, and the developmental curriculum (which environments appear and how task complexity ramps over a lifetime). Evolution tunes those priors; development runs them.

Operon Mapping: Evo-Devo → PatternLibrary

PatternLibrary serves as Operon’s evolutionary memory, implementing the outer loop. Each PatternTemplate plays the role of the genome φ (specifying topology, stage specs, and intervention policy), and each PatternRunRecord is a fitness evaluation. Because success_rate feeds directly into top_templates_for(), templates that perform reliably across many organism runs (“lifetimes”) rise in the rankings.

Emergent Cognitive Modes

The paper argues that even a fixed System M can support learning behaviors typically associated with large-brained species:

  • Learning through communication. System M’s species-specific signals include attention to pedagogical cues (direct gaze, pointing, infant-directed speech). This enables social learning: attending selectively to informative teachers and weighting their signals by past reliability, a form of epistemic vigilance that calibrates trust to track record.
  • Learning through imagination. During periods of low external stimulation (sleep, rest), System M can redirect data flow from external sensors to internal memory so episodic traces are replayed, consolidated, and compressed into schemas. Using System A’s world model, the agent can also simulate counterfactuals (“what would have happened if I had chosen differently?”).

Neither mode is implemented in v0.21; they are slated for Phase 5 (SleepConsolidation and counterfactual_replay() over bi-temporal memory) and Phase 6 (SocialLearning on top of QuorumSensing). Both depend on the scaffolding added here (a hardwired System M policy and evolutionary template memory): sleep consolidation requires a watcher that knows when to trigger it.

3. The Pattern Repository

PatternLibrary stores PatternTemplate instances (blueprints specifying a topology, stage specifications, and default intervention policies) and retrieves them by matching against a TaskFingerprint. 

from operon_ai import PatternLibrary, PatternTemplate, TaskFingerprint

lib = PatternLibrary()

# Register a template
lib.register_template(PatternTemplate(
    template_id=lib.make_id(),
    name="Enterprise Review",
    topology="skill_organism",
    stage_specs=(
        {"name": "research", "role": "Researcher", "mode": "fuzzy"},
        {"name": "strategy", "role": "Strategist", "mode": "deep"},
        {"name": "critique", "role": "Critic", "mode": "fuzzy"},
    ),
    intervention_policy={"max_retries": 2},
    fingerprint=TaskFingerprint(
        task_shape="sequential", tool_count=4,
        subtask_count=3, required_roles=("researcher", "strategist", "critic"),
    ),
))

# Later: retrieve ranked matches for a new task
ranked = lib.top_templates_for(TaskFingerprint(
    task_shape="sequential", tool_count=3,
    subtask_count=4, required_roles=("researcher", "analyst"),
))

Scoring

The top_templates_for() method scores each template using a weighted combination of six factors:

FactorWeightMetric
Task shape0.30Exact match (1.0 or 0.0)
Tool count0.151 / (1 + |delta|)
Subtask count0.151 / (1 + |delta|)
Role overlap0.20Jaccard similarity
Tag overlap0.10Jaccard similarity
Success rate0.10Historical win rate (default 0.5)

The success-rate term closes the selection loop from §2: as PatternRunRecord entries accumulate, reliable templates climb in score and failing ones drop, mapping template definitions to φ and run records to fitness evaluations.

4. The Watcher Component

WatcherComponent is a SkillRuntimeComponent that observes stage execution and sorts runtime signals into the three meta-state categories from §2:

  1. Epistemic: epiplexity and prediction error from EpiplexityMonitor. Low epiplexity indicates the agent is repeating itself without making progress (stagnation).
  2. Somatic: ATP and metabolic state from ATP_Store, signaling when the budget is nearly spent.
  3. Species-specific: immune threats and membrane alerts from ImmuneSystem, flagging confirmed or critical deviations from baseline that look adversarial.

Matching Interventions to Signal Type

Each category maps to a different response: epistemic stagnation calls for escalating to a stronger model, somatic depletion calls for conservation or halting, and immune threats require immediate quarantine. Collapsing them into a single “health score” throws away the signal needed to pick the right action.

5. The Intervention Mechanism

When the watcher decides to intervene, it writes a WatcherIntervention to shared_state under a reserved key. The organism’s run loop checks for this key after all component on_stage_result hooks complete:

InterventionRun Loop Behavior
RETRYRe-execute the current stage. The retry result replaces the original.
ESCALATERe-execute using the deep nucleus. Only applies to agent-backed stages.
HALTBreak the stage loop immediately. No further stages execute.

Component hooks are not re-invoked after a retry or escalation, which prevents recursive intervention loops: the replacement result stands, and hooks fire normally on the next stage. 

from operon_ai import WatcherComponent, WatcherConfig, skill_organism

watcher = WatcherComponent(
    config=WatcherConfig(max_intervention_rate=0.5),
    epiplexity_monitor=monitor,  # optional
    budget=atp_store,            # optional
)

organism = skill_organism(
    stages=[...],
    fast_nucleus=fast,
    deep_nucleus=deep,
    components=[watcher, telemetry],
)

6. Routing Decisions as a Failure Signal (BIGMAS)

The halting rule draws on a second March 2026 paper: “Brain-Inspired Graph Multi-Agent Systems for LLM Reasoning” by Hao, Dai, Qin, and Yu (Chinese Academy of Sciences). Their framework, BIGMAS, adapts Global Workspace Theory (GWT) to multi-agent reasoning and reports an empirical pattern that translates directly into a stopping condition for Operon’s watcher.

Global Workspace Theory in Multi-Agent Systems

GWT, originally a theory of human consciousness, posits that flexible cognition arises from dynamic coalitions of distributed specialized processors coordinated through a central workspace. BIGMAS translates this into three components for LLM reasoning:

  1. Problem-adaptive graph design. Rather than using a fixed topology, a GraphDesigner agent inspects each problem and constructs a task-specific directed graph whose nodes are specialized roles (generator, validator, analyzer, optimizer) and whose edges define information flow.
  2. Centralized workspace. Agents communicate only through a shared workspace with four partitions (read-only problem context, read-write intermediate work, execution metadata, and final answer), with no point-to-point messaging.
  3. Global orchestrator. A routing agent reads the full workspace state and execution history before selecting the next node, avoiding the partial-information bottleneck of reactive loops like ReAct.

Operon Mapping: BIGMAS Architecture

BIGMAS’s workspace partitions align with Operon’s three-layer context model: Bctx (problem context) maps to the topology layer, Bwork (intermediate results) to the shared_state ephemeral layer, and Bsys (execution metadata) to telemetry and watcher signals. The orchestrator corresponds to WatcherComponent, since both condition routing decisions on global state rather than local observation.

The Empirical Finding

Evaluated across six frontier LLMs (DeepSeek-V3.2, Claude 4.5 Sonnet, Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-5) on three combinatorial reasoning benchmarks, BIGMAS improved accuracy consistently (lifting GPT-5 from 91% to 98% on Tower of London), and those gains were orthogonal to model-level reasoning upgrades. Applying BIGMAS to LRMs (models with extended chain-of-thought) added further gains on top of thinking mode, indicating that architectural coordination complements individual model capability.

For Operon, the most directly useful result came from the routing analysis, which counted how many orchestrator decisions each run took:

TaskCorrect Runs (mean decisions)Incorrect Runs (mean decisions)
Game241.11.7
Six Fives2.94.6
Tower of London7.39.4

Incorrect runs consistently required more routing decisions than successful ones across all tasks. The orchestrator continued cycling through nodes in unsuccessful attempts, accumulating calls without converging. The paper notes this as an “emergent proxy for instance-level difficulty and non-convergence” and suggests it could inform early-stopping mechanisms.

Implementing the Stopping Rule

Operon’s watcher encodes this directly in _check_convergence(), which tracks the ratio of cumulative interventions (retries plus escalations) to observed stages. Once that ratio exceeds max_intervention_rate (default 0.5), the watcher emits a non-convergence HALT. It takes roughly 20 lines in the decision function: an organism that repeatedly needs correction is failing to converge, so stopping early beats burning budget on more retries.

Recording the halted run in PatternLibrary then lowers the template’s score for future tasks with similar fingerprints, feeding the watcher’s non-convergence signal back into template selection.

Architectural Differences from BIGMAS

Operon adopts the convergence signal while keeping a different runtime architecture. Whereas BIGMAS routes everything through a centralized workspace and a global orchestrator, Operon relies on distributed component hooks where the watcher inspects stage results sequentially. Instead of synthesizing a fresh graph for every problem instance and validating workspace writes through a dedicated protocol, Operon retrieves templates from a scored library and enforces integrity via stage-level type coercion and bi-temporal append-only writes.

Those choices reflect different cost trade-offs. BIGMAS optimizes for single-problem reasoning depth; the paper reports 18-37% of total tokens spent on graph design alone, which pays off on hard combinatorial puzzles. Operon optimizes for repeated enterprise workflows where one topology fits many similar tasks, keeping selection cost to a dictionary lookup and scoring pass.

Adaptation Cost Trade-Off

BIGMAS and Operon occupy different points on the adaptation-cost spectrum: high per-problem outlay for bespoke structure versus amortized adaptation across runs via the pattern library. A natural Phase 4 extension is a hybrid that runs BIGMAS-style graph design on novel tasks and registers the winning graphs as Operon templates for later reuse.

7. Decision Priority

The watcher evaluates signals in a fixed priority order:

  1. Intervention rate exceeded → HALT (non-convergence)
  2. Critical immune threat → HALT
  3. Critical epiplexity → ESCALATE (or HALT if already deep)
  4. Stagnant epiplexity on fast model → ESCALATE
  5. Stage failure with retries remaining → RETRY
  6. Otherwise → no intervention

Signals are evaluated lexicographically by severity rather than combined into a weighted score, so a critical immune threat always triggers HALT even when epiplexity looks healthy.

8. Zero Overhead When Not Attached

Both new components are opt-in. An organism with no WatcherComponent in its components list never checks for intervention keys, and PatternLibrary is a standalone object with no coupling to the run loop. Existing tests (987 from v0.20) pass without modification.

9. Closing the Loop: Adaptive Assembly (v0.21.1)

With the scaffolding in place, v0.21.1 closes the adaptive loop. The AdaptiveSkillOrganism wrapper composes the full lifecycle: fingerprint → retrieve → assemble → run → record. 

from operon_ai import adaptive_skill_organism, PatternLibrary, TaskFingerprint

lib = PatternLibrary()
# ... register templates ...

adaptive = adaptive_skill_organism(
    "Prepare a Q4 earnings analysis.",
    fingerprint=TaskFingerprint(
        task_shape="sequential", tool_count=3, subtask_count=3,
        required_roles=("researcher", "strategist"),
    ),
    library=lib,
    fast_nucleus=fast,
    deep_nucleus=deep,
    handlers={"intake": intake_fn, "research": research_fn, "strategy": strategy_fn},
)

result = adaptive.run("Prepare a Q4 earnings analysis.")
# result.template — which template was selected
# result.record — PatternRunRecord stored in library
# result.watcher_summary — signal/intervention counts

The factory adaptive_skill_organism() auto-fingerprints the task if no fingerprint is provided, queries PatternLibrary.top_templates_for() for the best template, and calls assemble_pattern() to dispatch on topology (skill_organism, reviewer_gate, specialist_swarm, or single_worker), attaching a WatcherComponent and TelemetryProbe automatically.

Experience Pool

After each run, the wrapper records a PatternRunRecord in the library (closing the scoring feedback loop) and populates the watcher’s experience pool with ExperienceRecord instances for each intervention. The experience pool persists across runs: when rule-based decision logic returns no intervention, the watcher consults past experiences with matching (stage, signal category, fingerprint shape) and recommends the intervention kind that was most often successful.

Rule-based decisions always take priority, with the experience pool acting as a fallback. An empty pool leaves behavior unchanged for backward compatibility, while accumulated runs let the watcher draw on operational history.

Lamarckian Refinement Over the Evo-Devo Loop

Each organism run is one developmental lifetime, and library scoring across runs acts as evolutionary selection over templates. The experience pool adds a Lamarckian layer on top, tracking which interventions succeed within those templates. Together, the releases follow the roadmap progression of structure → memory → adaptation: explicit structure in v0.17–0.18, auditable memory in v0.19–0.20, and runtime adaptation in v0.21.

11. What Comes Next

v0.21 ships both the scaffolding (v0.21.0) and the adaptive loop (v0.21.1). With patterns stored, scored, and assembled automatically, and interventions refined from operational history, the next phases target the cognitive extensions Dupoux, LeCun, and Malik describe for a working System M.

Phase 5 implements learning through imagination: SleepConsolidation replays episodic memories from bi-temporal storage, compresses successful patterns into templates, and runs counterfactual_replay() to test what would have happened under different facts. Phase 6 implements learning through communication: SocialLearning extends QuorumSensing so organisms can adopt templates that worked for peers, using epistemic vigilance (trust calibrated to track record) to filter unreliable sources.

Autonomous learning needs all three systems wired together: observation without action lacks grounding, action without observation lacks abstraction, and meta-control has nothing to steer without both. v0.21 is the first release with all three pieces connected, combining template selection, run-loop intervention, and the experience pool in one runtime so Phases 5 and 6 have a concrete base to build on.
