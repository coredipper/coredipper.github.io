SLAM Already Solved Stagnation

operon-langgraph-gates v0.1 is a discrete-state port of factor-graph fixed-lag smoothing (Kaess 2012). Writing the mapping down is straightforward; what matters is the scope discipline it enforces.
Bogdan Banu · April 23, 2026
operon-langgraph-gates v0.1.0 (stable, 2026-04-30)

A LangGraph user filed issue #6731 asking for protection against infinite agent loops. The maintainers closed it as NOT_PLANNED: out of scope for the framework.

I do not blame them for treating it as out of scope. Still, it was the most frequent complaint across the four sources of LangGraph issues, discussions, and blog posts I reviewed before starting operon-langgraph-gates. Four sources do not make a census, though they were enough to convince me that agents failing to notice when they have stopped making progress is a real reliability gap in production LangGraph deployments.

Robotics already has a standard pattern for this exact failure shape. Factor-graph SLAM has relied on it since the mid-2000s, formalised incrementally in iSAM2 (Kaess et al. 2012): fixed-lag smoothing over a factor graph, which is how modern SLAM back-ends track whether a state estimate is improving or stuck.

Frank Dellaert just published a blog post framing factor graphs as a structured instance of the energy-based “world models” explored in the JEPA literature. He calls the loop STAG (Sense, Think, Act, with Graphs): a past graph handles perception via fixed-lag smoothing, a future graph handles planning via predictive control, and both minimise energy over a shared dynamics model.

Reading that post the same morning I was writing the README for operon-langgraph-gates made something obvious that had been sitting in the code for a year: Operon’s runtime guards have the graph structure of a discrete-state STAG loop. The pre-guard checks dynamics residuals against a standing checkpoint, the windowed stability verifier acts as the past-graph smoother, and the post-guard is a degenerate future-graph planner. To borrow the phrasing from the companion paper appendix, the guards admit a STAG description even though the implementation does not run a factor-graph solver.

What follows is the term-by-term correspondence and the theoretical basis behind operon-langgraph-gates v0.1.

Relation to the cert-firing post

Two days ago, in Score-Rejection Isn’t Cert-Firing (and n=10 Isn’t Enough), I separated two signals that are easy to conflate during evaluation: a certificate fires only when a named structural property is attested, whereas a score rejection just means a scalar crossed a threshold. This post applies the same distinction one layer deeper, to the detection mechanism itself: what makes a stagnation detector structural rather than purely algorithmic?

Factor graphs give a concrete answer because you cannot even write the loop down until you specify three things: the state space, the observations, and the dynamics model. Every post here since What Biological Agent Design Actually Buys You has argued for structural guarantees over ad-hoc heuristics, and factor graphs show what that commitment looks like in explicit math.

What LangGraph users are asking for

Issue #6731 makes a narrow, practical request: halt the agent when it stops making progress. Users are not asking for a learned world model or a general termination proof; they want the robot-operator rule that stops execution when the state estimate has not moved over the last W steps.

Anyone who runs agents recognises the failure immediately: an LLM retries the same failing tool call six times in a row, or talks itself into calling tool X and then never calls it. The pattern is obvious to a human watching the logs; the hard part is detecting it in the runtime before hitting the budget cap.

LangChain’s answer, which is defensible for a general orchestration library, is post-hoc observability: inspect the trace in LangSmith, notice the loop, and tighten the prompt. That works for one-off debugging, though it leaves runtime reliability unaddressed when a standard detection pattern already exists in robotics.

A brief recap of factor graphs

A factor graph has variables (what you care about) and factors (functions that score combinations of variables). The joint score is the product of factor values. For robotics, the variables are states at different times xt−5, …, xt. The factors come in two flavours:

  • Measurement factors score how well a state matches a sensor reading.
  • Dynamics factors score how well a state transition matches the motion model.

Fixed-lag smoothing anchors the graph at the current time t and keeps the last W steps in memory. Every time a new observation arrives, you slide the window, add one measurement factor, add one dynamics factor, marginalise the oldest variable into a prior factor, and re-solve. You get a rolling MAP estimate of where the robot has been. If that estimate stops moving, the robot is stuck.

Dellaert’s STAG framing adds a forward-looking side anchored at the same state xt, introducing goal factors and control variables. Perception is energy minimisation on the past graph, planning is energy minimisation on the future graph, and both halves share one dynamics model glued at the present.

This formulation has held up for 20 years in production robotics because it leaves no room to hand-wave: every component corresponds to an explicit state variable, an observation model, or a transition law.

The translation to LLM agents

Walking through the mapping term by term shows both what carries over cleanly and where the analogy stops.

State. In Operon, xt is the agent’s symbolic tuple of (genome, expression, short-term memory) between stages rather than a Euclidean vector, and the transition dynamics are a deterministic checkpoint prediction rather than a learned model.

Measurement factor (with one caveat). In operon_ai/core/certificate.py, the behavioral_stability_windowed verifier computes a per-window residual r = max(0, x − τ), where x is the per-window mean of a quality signal and τ is the stability threshold. Each window’s “measurement factor” is the indicator 1[r = 0], which collapses a likelihood factor into a hard admissibility constraint. Operon inherits the graph topology without the probabilistic semantics: there is no Mahalanobis residual, no information matrix, and no covariance on the smoothed belief. That is a real loss, and it is the first place a SLAM reader should push back.

Dynamics factor. DNARepair.scan in operon_ai/state/dna_repair.py checks whether the observed genome hash matches the checkpoint’s predicted hash. That is the dynamics residual, with the checkpoint serving as the deterministic dynamics model. If the residual is positive, the agent has transitioned into an inadmissible state and the pre-guard halts.

Past graph (the windowed verifier). The windowed stability verifier is the component that corresponds to a fixed-lag smoother, aggregating a sequence of per-window residuals into a pass/fail verdict over the window. The pre_guard in compile_guarded_graph (operon_ai/convergence/guarded_graph.py) is a separate one-step check running DNARepair.scan against the standing checkpoint. It is easy to conflate the two: the pre-guard evaluates a single-step dynamics residual, while the windowed verifier plays the smoother role across a rolling window.

Future graph. In the same file, the post-guard runs a rubric verifier and either accepts the output or routes to a retry. That gives a single goal factor at horizon h=1: a deliberately degenerate version of STAG’s future graph. Pushing past h > 1 turns the guard into a multi-step planner, which is where agent systems usually fall apart.

Shared dynamics. Both guards share the coalgebraic state transition defined in operon_ai/core/coalgebra.py, anchoring the past and future checks to a single dynamics model at the current stage.

Joining across agents (with the same caveat). When two agents exchange certificates via the A2A codec (operon_ai/convergence/a2a_certificate.py), they treat each certificate as shared evidence conditioned on schema agreement: the receiver can verify the predicate locally and fold it in, or forward it unverified under the graceful-degradation rule. Unlike distributed SLAM, where agents negotiate agreement on continuous latent poses, Operon agents only share a predicate name and a boolean result. The monoidal structure of the optimiser is consistent with associative certificate composition, though I would not claim more than that without writing down the functor. It shares the topology of factor joining without its continuous probabilistic semantics.

That is the entire mapping: no new optimiser and no learned factors, just the observation that pre- and post-guards around an LLM have the same graph topology as the rolling estimation loop SLAM has used in front of cameras for fifteen years. As the appendix puts it, the correspondence gives a dual description rather than a free algorithmic speedup.

Why the correspondence is useful

Four practical consequences follow from making the mapping explicit:

1. Citation lineage. Describing the guard as a discrete-state analogue of fixed-lag smoothing (Kaess et al. 2012) is both more accurate about what the code does and easier for reviewers and reliability engineers to evaluate than calling it a custom stagnation check.

2. Structured composition. I originally thought of the A2A codec simply as a serialisation format with graceful degradation. Under the factor-graph view, it is shared evidence under a shared schema with the topology of factor joining: reliability gates across separate agents compose associatively via the schema and monoidal structure. Because the join operates over booleans rather than continuous latents, the algebra is strictly weaker than in distributed SLAM, though still useful if kept within those bounds.

3. Vocabulary for extensions. Every natural extension to operon-langgraph-gates (longer pre-guard windows, goal factors in the post-guard, richer checkpoint dynamics) now has a standard name. If someone proposes learning factors from data, the trade-off is immediately explicit: replacing a structural guarantee with an empirical one, which may be acceptable in some settings as long as the boundary stays visible.

4. A hard line against scope drift. Once you write down a factor graph, the obvious temptation is to start training the factors, which is the JEPA research programme. That is a reasonable direction, just outside what Operon and operon-langgraph-gates aim to do. Here the factors and topology stay fixed in code, and only the library of verified properties grows; fitting residuals to data would turn a structural guarantee back into an empirical heuristic.

Limits of the analogy

Three boundaries worth stating directly:

  • No claim of novelty. Fixed-lag smoothing is over 20 years old. Running a discrete version over symbolic LLM agent state with verification instead of gradient descent is a straightforward port.
  • Operon is not a world model. There are no learned dynamics, no predictive rollouts, and no JEPA-style energy function. The link to STAG is purely structural, with a residual count standing in for energy.
  • Theory alone does not fix LangGraph #6731. Factor graphs provide vocabulary and design discipline; actually closing the loop takes roughly 200 lines of runtime code in the gates repository.

What ships in v0.1

operon-langgraph-gates v0.1 ships two drop-in LangGraph nodes. StagnationGate wraps the rolling-window stagnation detector benchmarked in Paper 4 §4.3 at 0.960 on convergence and false-stagnation scenarios with real sentence embeddings, checking whether the smoothed state has moved over the last W steps. IntegrityGate checks user-defined invariants against a standing checkpoint as a dynamics-residual guard. Neither mentions “organism,” “skill,” or anything biological, because nobody wiring up a LangGraph agent wants a mitochondrial metaphor, and I have finally internalised that.

The README summarises the point plainly: these primitives are a port of one of the oldest, most battle-tested reliability loops in autonomy research. LLM agents simply grew up on a different vocabulary track from robotics, so the overlap went mostly unnoticed.
